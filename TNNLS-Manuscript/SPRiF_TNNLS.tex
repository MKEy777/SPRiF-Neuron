\begin{abstract}
Temporal tasks with long delays require spiking neural networks (SNNs) to retain information while continuing to communicate through spikes. A standard spiking neuron uses one membrane state to integrate context, generate spikes, and receive the reset that follows a spike, so each spike can disturb the temporal trace carried by that state. We introduce SPRiF, a five-state neuron that separates a spectral memory from the membrane that reads it. The memory combines a real decay with a damped rotation, and two learned projections drive the membrane, one immediately and one through an auxiliary filter delayed by one step. Because the reset acts only on the membrane, the spectral coordinates continue their evolution at discharge. We derive the input-to-drive kernel and show that a reset applied to spectral memory adds a separate spike-history term. Across five temporal classification benchmarks, SPRiF attains the highest reported mean accuracy among the compared methods on three tasks and remains competitive on the other two. Matched controls on permuted sequential MNIST and Spiking Heidelberg Digits isolate reset placement and mode organization: preserving memory at reset adds 3.37 and 4.56 percentage points over the memory-reset control, and retaining the rotational pair adds 2.34 and 2.47 over three driven real modes. On Spike-Intervention Delayed Match-to-Sample, SPRiF retains 99.42\% under 40 forced-spike timesteps after clean training and 99.33\% after training with up to eight intervention timesteps, averaged over five delays including an unseen 2500-ms delay. Impulse responses show heterogeneous learned temporal kernels.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{I}{n} a delayed decision, the first cue may be gone by the time the second arrives. Spikes emitted between the cues serve two roles in a recurrent spiking neural network (SNN). They reset the membranes that fired and enter the recurrent current on the next step. In the leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) model, an input pulse and a subsequent reset leave traces in the same membrane voltage, both governed by the same decay. The signal available at the second cue therefore depends on the intervening spikes as well as the first cue.

A neuron with several state variables can assign these roles differently. Adaptive neurons retain a history of activity~\cite{asrnn,adlif}, heterogeneous neurons and synapses distribute temporal integration across time scales~\cite{heterogeneity,hetsyn}, resonant dynamics produce lag-dependent responses~\cite{izhikevich_rf,brf,drf}, and coupled compartments distribute integration across states~\cite{tclif,dhsnn}. PMSN separates linear hidden compartments from a firing compartment~\cite{pmsn2026}, while DMP-SNN maintains a memory shared across a layer~\cite{sun2026dmp}. Reset itself can also be softened, adapted or removed~\cite{inflor,arlif,psn}. These developments motivate a joint question about the organization of a fixed number of states within one neuron: which temporal responses can the membrane read, and how does a spike change those responses?

SPRiF assigns three coordinates to a spectral memory, one to a filter and one to the membrane. A pulse excites a decaying trace and a damped rotation. The membrane reads a projection of the current spectral state and a filtered projection from the preceding step. These two paths allow the neuron to change the weight of older inputs while holding the instantaneous response fixed. The local reset acts on the membrane alone. For a specified current sequence, we derive the kernel that maps input to membrane drive and identify the extra spike-history term introduced when reset also acts on the spectral state.

We evaluate SPRiF on five temporal benchmarks and test the two design choices on permuted sequential MNIST (PS-MNIST) and Spiking Heidelberg Digits (SHD). Two controls retain the same five states, eleven learned local coefficients and layer widths. One adds a reset to spectral memory. The other replaces the spectral block with three driven real decays. A second experiment, Spike-Intervention Delayed Match-to-Sample (SI-DMS), forces spikes between two cues. It tests whether the full recurrent network can still compare the cues when its spike history changes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Temporal Dynamics in Spiking Neurons}

Adaptive neurons retain activity in a variable that influences later firing. ASRNN combines adaptive spiking dynamics with recurrent processing for time-domain classification~\cite{asrnn}, while the adLIF family, including the SE-adLIF comparator in Table~\ref{tab:main_results}, exploits adaptation for spatiotemporal processing~\cite{adlif}. SNN-SFA uses spike-frequency adaptation to retain information through a slowly varying firing threshold~\cite{snnsfa}. Earlier spikes leave a trace in neuronal excitability that can support the subsequent recall of temporally dispersed inputs. RadLIF instead combines recurrent connections with an adaptive current driven by subthreshold voltage and spike-triggered increments~\cite{radlif}. These models illustrate two ways to carry activity beyond the membrane time constant: changing the threshold for future firing or changing the current that opposes membrane integration.

DA-LIF introduces independently learnable decays for spatial and temporal tuning~\cite{dalif}. Temporal diversity can also be distributed across neurons: Heterogeneous SNN distributes neuronal parameters to support different integration times~\cite{heterogeneity}, and bimodal time-scale networks explicitly combine populations with short and long time constants~\cite{bimodal}. HetSyn instead assigns time constants to individual synapses, placing temporal integration in synaptic currents~\cite{hetsyn}. Conductance-based models obtain multiple time scales through fast, slow and ultra-slow conductances~\cite{mtc}. These approaches broaden the temporal dynamics available to a network by changing the variables that retain activity and the rates at which they evolve. For SPRiF, the design question also concerns the shape of the retained response. A real decay assigns a fixed-sign trace to an isolated pulse, whereas a rotational pair gives the readout access to its changing phase. Combining these components provides both persistence and phase-dependent weighting within one neuron.

Resonate-and-fire neurons use coupled states whose phase affects the response to a past input~\cite{izhikevich_rf}. BRF develops balanced resonate-and-fire dynamics for trainable spiking networks~\cite{brf}. PRF combines resonant membrane dynamics with a decoupled reset construction for parallel sequence processing~\cite{prf}. 

Compartmental models offer another organization: Dendrify provides reduced models with dendritic integration and local spikes~\cite{dendrify}, TC-LIF couples dendritic and somatic states~\cite{tclif}, and DH-SNN assigns heterogeneous time constants to dendritic branches~\cite{dhsnn}. A close relative of SPRiF is D-RF, whose resonant dendrites feed a somatic readout and whose threshold depends on spike history~\cite{drf}. Its dendrites also evolve without subtractive output reset. SPRiF gives the membrane two learned projections of its spectral states, with the second acting through an auxiliary filter one step later. This readout structure separates the immediate response from part of the delayed response: changing the filtered projection alters later membrane drive without changing its value at the arrival of an input pulse. The temporal kernel makes this distinction explicit.

State-space models motivate both the parameterization of spiking dynamics and their organization within a sequence model. SiLIF transfers learnable discretization and logarithmic parameterization to spiking neurons, with a complex-state variant that supports oscillatory regimes~\cite{silif}. SpikingSSMs combine state-space sequence processing with sparse spiking outputs~\cite{spikingssms}. SPRiF specifies a real pole and a complex-conjugate pair directly, then learns how their states reach the membrane through immediate and delayed projections.

\subsection{Reset Mechanisms and Spike History}

PMSN separates the dynamics of its hidden compartments from discharge at its output~\cite{pmsn2026}. Unrolling its diagonalized hidden transition gives a convolution from injected current to the drive at the output compartment. Its parallel output update requires a separate construction, temporal-decoupled reset, which operates on rectified current and discharges integer multiples of the threshold. SPRiF uses a leaky membrane with signed drive and one threshold subtraction per binary spike. This choice gives a closed form for the drive to the membrane while retaining a sequential firing decision. 

Reset design determines how firing changes the state available at later timesteps. Guo \emph{et al.} use a soft reset and membrane-potential rectification to reduce information loss~\cite{inflor}; AR-LIF adapts reset through the relation between input and output~\cite{arlif}. RPLIF models refractory effects through spike-triggered threshold dynamics~\cite{rplif}, while Zhang analyzes reset and refractory periods from the perspective of binary-activated recurrent networks~\cite{zhang2025reset}. PSN removes reset to obtain parallelizable temporal computations~\cite{psn}. SPRiF retains a subtractive membrane reset and analyzes which additional history term appears when that reset is extended to spectral memory.

NiSNN-A writes LIF voltage as the difference between accumulated input and accumulated reset~\cite{nisnna2025}. Its NiLIF update estimates the unknown spike train from the input-only accumulation to obtain a noniterative computation. In SPRiF, spikes are generated by thresholding the actual membrane candidate at each step. The local response to a realized spike can then be derived from the state equations.

\subsection{Gating and Memory Organization}

Gating provides another means of controlling how information enters and persists in a spiking neuron. GLIF learns gating factors that combine alternative biological features of membrane leakage, input integration and spike initiation~\cite{glif}. Its learned gates and membrane parameters enlarge the family of neuronal dynamics that a network can express. DGN introduces activity-dependent membrane conductance as a dynamic gate~\cite{dgn}. This mechanism selectively filters incoming signals and suppresses disturbances, linking neuronal activity to the strength of subsequent input transmission. Both approaches make the regulation of information flow an explicit part of neuron design. SPRiF instead specifies a linear spectral memory and learns its projections into the membrane, with the threshold nonlinearity applied at the output.

Temporal coordination can also be studied at the network level. MPS-SNN views successive timesteps as temporal subnetworks with shared architectures and weights~\cite{mpssnn}. It uses membrane-potential smoothing and guidance between adjacent temporal subnetworks to reduce inconsistencies in their states and outputs. The smoothing mechanism also supports the propagation of information and gradients through time. This perspective emphasizes consistency across successive network evaluations. The SPRiF analysis focuses on the response retained inside each neuron: how an input pulse is transformed over time and how an intervening spike changes the drive available to later decisions.

DMP-SNN makes a different architectural choice by sharing memory within a layer~\cite{sun2026dmp}. Layer-level sharing and neuron-local state organization provide different ways to allocate a network's memory resources. SPRiF addresses the latter by specifying how five local states receive input, communicate with the membrane and respond to firing. Its matched controls keep the number of states and learned dynamics coefficients fixed, so the comparison focuses on their temporal modes and reset placement.

Together, these studies motivate separating the temporal response carried by a state from the changes imposed on it by firing. SPRiF examines both within one neuron: its spectral block determines the basis available to the membrane, and its reset rule determines whether local spikes directly modify that basis state. This distinction also separates local memory reset from recurrent feedback. A spike can leave the memory coordinates unchanged at discharge and still alter their future trajectory through the recurrent input. The theoretical analysis distinguishes these two paths, while SI-DMS evaluates their combined effect on delayed decisions.

\section{SPRiF Neuron Model and Analysis}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Neuron Formulation}
\label{sec:motivation}
\label{sec:forward}

To fix the timing convention, let $v_t$ be a LIF membrane immediately after the current reset. Its candidate, spike, and stored voltage are
\begin{equation}
\widetilde v_t=\beta v_{t-1}+I_t,\qquad
z_t=H(\widetilde v_t-\theta),\qquad
v_t=\widetilde v_t-\theta z_t,
\label{eq:lif}
\end{equation}
where $0<\beta<1$, $\theta>0$, and $H(a)=1$ for $a\geq0$ and zero otherwise. Unrolling gives
\begin{equation}
v_t=\beta^{t+1}v_{-1}
+\sum_{j=0}^{t}\beta^{t-j} I_j
-\theta\sum_{j=0}^{t}\beta^{t-j}z_j.
\label{eq:lif_unrolled}
\end{equation}
A unit of current at step $j$ contributes $\beta^{t-j}$ at step $t$. A spike at the same step contributes $-\theta\beta^{t-j}$. The two contributions differ in sign and scale but have the same time course. SPRiF retains membrane reset and gives the membrane access to a second history, a filtered record of the input current whose coordinates are preserved at discharge.

For an input vector $\mathbf{s}_t$ and a recurrent spiking layer, the current vector is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf I_t=W_{\mathrm{in}}\mathbf s_t+
W_{\mathrm{rec}}\mathbf z_{t-1}+\mathbf b.
\label{eq:current}
\end{equation}
The recurrent term is omitted for a feedforward layer. The equations below concern one neuron and suppress its index. Its current $I_t$ is the corresponding component of $\mathbf I_t$, and it stores $\mathbf x_t\in\R^3$, $h_t\in\R$, and $u_t^0\in\R$:
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf x_t&=A\mathbf x_{t-1}+BI_t,\\
h_t&=\eta_1h_{t-1}+\bar{\mathbf g}_1^\top\mathbf x_t,\\
\widetilde u_t^0&=\eta_0u_{t-1}^0+h_{t-1}
                    +\mathbf g_0^\top\mathbf x_t,\\
z_t&=H(\widetilde u_t^0-\theta),\\
u_t^0&=\widetilde u_t^0-\theta z_t.
\end{aligned}
\right.
\label{eq:sprif}
\end{equation}
The transition and input vector are
\begin{equation}
A=\begin{bmatrix}
\alpha&0&0\\
0&\rho\cos\omega&-\rho\sin\omega\\
0&\rho\sin\omega&\rho\cos\omega
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
B=\begin{bmatrix}1-\alpha\\1-\rho\\0\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:AB}
\end{equation}
The two directly learned readouts form
\begin{equation}
\bar G=
\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf g_0^\top\\\bar{\mathbf g}_1^\top\end{bmatrix}
\in\R^{2\times3}.
\label{eq:G}
\end{equation}
For finite raw parameters, the constraints are enforced by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\alpha&=\sigma(\alpha_{\rm raw}),&
\rho&=\sigma(\rho_{\rm raw}),\\
\omega&=\pi\sigma(\omega_{\rm raw}),&
\eta_i&=\sigma(\eta_{i,{\rm raw}}),\quad i\in\{0,1\}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:constraints}
\end{equation}
Here $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid. Thus $\alpha,\rho,\eta_i\in(0,1)$ and $\omega\in(0,\pi)$. The six entries of $\bar G$ are unconstrained real coefficients. The threshold is a fixed task setting.

Figure~\ref{fig:organization} contrasts the state organization of LIF and SPRiF. In SPRiF, two routes connect $\mathbf x_t$ to firing. The direct projection enters $\widetilde u_t^0$ immediately. The second projection updates $h_t$, which first enters the membrane at $t+1$. At the current step the membrane reads $h_{t-1}$. All intrinsic states start at zero for an independent sequence.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\columnwidth]{figure/lif_sprif_overview.png}
\caption{State organization of LIF (left) and SPRiF (right). LIF integrates input and reset in one membrane. SPRiF separates spectral memory from the auxiliary filter and membrane, with spike-triggered reset confined to the membrane.}
\label{fig:organization}
\end{figure}

The filter-to-membrane coupling in Eq.~\eqref{eq:sprif} has unit gain. A separate constant gain $\kappa$ would be redundant with the unconstrained input projection of the filter. For a filter $u_t^1=\eta_1u_{t-1}^1+\mathbf g_1^\top\mathbf x_t$ read out as $\kappa u_{t-1}^1$, set
\begin{equation}
h_t=\kappa u_t^1,\qquad
\bar{\mathbf g}_1=\kappa\mathbf g_1,\qquad
h_{-1}=\kappa u_{-1}^1 .
\label{eq:absorption}
\end{equation}
Substitution gives the same forward membrane voltage and spikes for fixed coefficients, including at $\kappa=0$. SPRiF learns $\bar{\mathbf g}_1$ directly. This leaves five decay or frequency parameters and six projection coefficients per neuron.

Each neuron stores $(x^0,x^1,x^2,h,u^0)$, for five state values. It learns the five raw variables associated with $\alpha,\rho,\omega,\eta_0,\eta_1$ and six entries of $\bar G$, for eleven dynamics coefficients. Thresholds are fixed, and synaptic weights, biases, and output parameters are separate.

For a recurrent layer with input width $m$ and hidden width $n$, a dense input matrix, dense recurrent matrix, and one current bias per neuron contribute
\begin{equation}
N_{\rm layer}=mn+n^2+n+11n.
\label{eq:parameters}
\end{equation}
Complete network counts include the task readout and use the bias settings of the corresponding layer. A width-$n$ layer stores $5n$ intrinsic state values in addition to its spike cache. Its local update takes $O(n)$ operations per step, while the dense synaptic products take $O(mn+n^2)$.

\subsection{Temporal Kernel Analysis}
\label{sec:kernels}

The poles of the spectral transition are
\begin{equation}
\spec(A)=\{\alpha,\rho e^{\mathrm i\omega},
                       \rho e^{-\mathrm i\omega}\}.
\label{eq:poles}
\end{equation}
For a unit impulse $I_0=1$, zero state at $t=-1$, and zero later current,
\begin{equation}
A^kB=
\begin{bmatrix}
(1-\alpha)\alpha^k\\
(1-\rho)\rho^k\cos(k\omega)\\
(1-\rho)\rho^k\sin(k\omega)
\end{bmatrix},\qquad k\geq0.
\label{eq:impulse}
\end{equation}
The first coordinate retains one sign. In the other two, $\rho$ sets the decay per step and $\omega$ sets the phase advance. For example, at $\omega=\pi/2$ the cosine coordinate equals $1-\rho$ at lag zero and $-(1-\rho)\rho^2$ two steps later. A projection onto that coordinate can therefore give the same pulse opposite weights according to its age.

The auxiliary filter accumulates a second projection of this trajectory. For the same pulse its response is
\begin{equation}
h_k=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\eta_1^{k-\ell}
                \bar{\mathbf g}_1^\top A^\ell B.
\label{eq:h_kernel}
\end{equation}
Write the membrane drive as
\begin{equation}
d_t=\mathbf g_0^\top\mathbf x_t+h_{t-1}.
\label{eq:drive}
\end{equation}
Substituting the spectral and filter responses gives the kernel
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
k_d[k]={}&\mathbf g_0^\top A^kB\\
&{}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\eta_1^{k-1-\ell}
                       \bar{\mathbf g}_1^\top A^\ell B ,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:drive_kernel}
\end{equation}
The sum is empty at $k=0$. In particular, $k_d[0]=\mathbf g_0^\top B$ and $k_d[1]=\mathbf g_0^\top AB+\bar{\mathbf g}_1^\top B$. Adjusting the second projection can change the response one step after a pulse while leaving its onset unchanged. Writing $\xi$ for a unit delay gives the transfer function
\begin{equation}
\mathcal K_d(\xi)=
\left(\mathbf g_0^\top+
\frac{\xi}{1-\eta_1\xi}\bar{\mathbf g}_1^\top\right)
(I_3-\xi A)^{-1}B.
\label{eq:transfer}
\end{equation}
For fixed coefficients and zero initial state, convolving $k_d$ with the realized current sequence gives the exact drive. The membrane then integrates this drive and its own reset history. For any initial membrane voltage,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
u_t^0={}&\eta_0^{t+1}u_{-1}^0
+\sum_{j=0}^{t}\eta_0^{t-j}d_j\\
&{}-\theta\sum_{j=0}^{t}\eta_0^{t-j}z_j .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:membrane_unrolled}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:membrane_unrolled} assigns the membrane its own leak $\eta_0$. The drive that precedes this integration has the poles of the spectral block and the auxiliary filter, subject to cancellation by the learned projections.

The complex pair contributes the quadratic factor $1-2\rho\cos(\omega)\xi+\rho^2\xi^2$ to the denominator of Eq.~\eqref{eq:transfer}. If this factor survives in the readout, a block containing only positive real decays and the same auxiliary filter cannot reproduce the kernel at all lags. Its denominator has only real linear factors. The distinction is an intrinsic oscillatory mode, since signed sums of real decays can themselves change sign. Section~\ref{sec:controls} defines a control with three driven real modes.

\subsection{Controllability and State Boundedness}
\label{sec:stability}

Current enters two spectral coordinates directly (Eq.~\eqref{eq:AB}). Rotation couples one of them to the third. The resulting three-dimensional state can be reached with a scalar current.

\begin{proposition}[Driven spectral coordinates]
For $\alpha,\rho\in(0,1)$ and $\omega\in(0,\pi)$, the pair $(A,B)$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:AB} is controllable.
\label{prop:controllability}
\end{proposition}
\begin{IEEEproof}
Let $\mathcal C=[B,AB,A^2B]$. Expanding its determinant gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\det\mathcal C={}&(1-\alpha)(1-\rho)^2\rho\sin\omega\\
&{}\times(\alpha^2-2\alpha\rho\cos\omega+\rho^2).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:controllability}
\end{equation}
Every factor is positive, since the last factor equals
$|\alpha-\rho e^{\mathrm i\omega}|^2$. Thus $\mathcal C$ has rank three.
\end{IEEEproof}

From zero state, three appropriately chosen scalar inputs can therefore reach any point in the spectral state space. Reachability concerns the state before projection. A learned readout can suppress a reachable mode, and a nearly singular controllability matrix can make some directions difficult to excite.

For fixed parameters and a specified current sequence, define
$\mathbf m_t=[\mathbf x_t^\top,h_t]^\top$. The memory pathway is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf m_t=F\mathbf m_{t-1}+LI_t,
\label{eq:memory_system}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
F=
\begin{bmatrix}
A&\mathbf0\\
\bar{\mathbf g}_1^\top A&\eta_1
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
L=
\begin{bmatrix}B\\\bar{\mathbf g}_1^\top B\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:memory_matrices}
\end{equation}
The block-triangular structure gives
\begin{equation}
\spec(F)=\{\alpha,\rho e^{\mathrm i\omega},
               \rho e^{-\mathrm i\omega},\eta_1\}.
\label{eq:F_poles}
\end{equation}
Each pole is strictly inside the unit disk. The learned projection into $h_t$ can amplify a transient even though every mode decays.

\begin{proposition}[Bounded local states]
Assume fixed finite readouts, finite initial states, $\theta>0$, the ranges in Eq.~\eqref{eq:constraints}, and $|I_t|\leq M$ for all $t\geq0$. Then $\mathbf x_t$, $h_t$, and $u_t^0$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:sprif} remain bounded.
\label{prop:boundedness}
\end{proposition}
\begin{IEEEproof}
Let $q=\max(\alpha,\rho)<1$. The rotational part of $A^k$ is a rotation scaled by $\rho^k$, so $\|A^k\|_2=q^k$. Summing the geometric series for the forced spectral recurrence gives a uniform bound $X$ on $\|\mathbf x_t\|_2$. Applying the same argument to the filter gives a bound $H_*$ on $|h_t|$, where
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
X &= \|\mathbf x_{-1}\|_2 + \frac{\|B\|_2 M}{1-q},\\
H_* &= |h_{-1}| + \frac{\|\bar{\mathbf g}_1\|_2 X}{1-\eta_1}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:local_bounds}
\end{equation}
It follows that $|d_t|\leq \|\mathbf g_0\|_2X+H_*$. Since each spike subtracts at most $\theta$, the expansion in Eq.~\eqref{eq:membrane_unrolled} yields
\begin{equation}
|u_t^0|\leq |u_{-1}^0|+
\frac{\|\mathbf g_0\|_2 X+H_*+\theta}{1-\eta_0}.
\label{eq:u_bound}
\end{equation}
All three bounds are finite.
\end{IEEEproof}

For a prescribed current sequence, a perturbation within the memory and filter coordinates propagates as $\delta\mathbf m_{t+k}=F^k\delta\mathbf m_t$. Poles near the unit circle permit slow decay along this path. If a spike changes a subsequent current, an additional input-driven response must be included.

\subsection{Reset Effects on Memory Dynamics}
\label{sec:reset}

Use superscripts $-$ and $+$ for values immediately before and after the current local reset. Equation~\eqref{eq:sprif} gives
\begin{equation}
\mathbf x_t^+=\mathbf x_t^-,
\qquad h_t^+=h_t^-,
\qquad u_t^{0,+}=\widetilde u_t^0-\theta z_t.
\label{eq:reset_identity}
\end{equation}
A spike changes stored voltage immediately. All four memory and filter coordinates have zero reset increment, so for a given current sequence the drive $d_t$ contains no direct contribution from past local resets.

To see what changes when a spike also acts on memory, consider the control in Eq.~\eqref{eq:memory_reset_control}. After both projections have read $\mathbf x_j^-$, a spike at $j$ adds $-\theta\widehat B$ to the stored spectral state, where $\widehat B=B/\|B\|_2$. An input pulse of size $-\theta/\|B\|_2$ would produce the same spectral displacement. Its timing within the step would differ, however, because an input pulse enters $h_j$ before the reset occurs. Under a unit pulse, that filter update contributes $\eta_1^{k-1}\bar{\mathbf g}_1^\top B$ to the drive at lag $k\geq1$. Removing this contribution from Eq.~\eqref{eq:drive_kernel} and scaling by the signed pulse amplitude gives the kernel $r$ from a spike to the membrane drive. The current drive has already been computed, so $r[0]=0$. At later lags,
\begin{equation}
r[k]=-\frac{\theta}{\|B\|_2}
\left(k_d[k]-\eta_1^{k-1}\bar{\mathbf g}_1^\top B\right),
\quad k\geq1 .
\label{eq:reset_kernel}
\end{equation}
For zero initial states, superposition in the memory and filter recurrences now gives
\begin{equation}
d_t=\sum_{k=0}^{t} k_d[k]\,I_{t-k}
    +\sum_{k=1}^{t} r[k]\,z_{t-k}.
\label{eq:drive_histories}
\end{equation}
The second sum is the direct contribution of memory reset in \mr{}. In SPRiF its kernel is identically zero by Eq.~\eqref{eq:reset_identity}. Each identity uses the model's own fixed coefficients and realized currents and spikes. The membrane in both models still receives its scalar reset.

A spike can also return through the network. Holding the external layer input fixed, a change in the spike vector at $t$ changes the next recurrent current by
\begin{equation}
\delta\mathbf I_{t+1}=W_{\rm rec}\,\delta\mathbf z_t .
\label{eq:recurrent_feedback}
\end{equation}
This change enters the first sum of Eq.~\eqref{eq:drive_histories} through the same kernel as any other current. It is distinct from the direct memory reset in the second sum. SI-DMS tests the joint effect of overwriting membrane candidates, firing, reset and recurrent propagation on a delayed decision.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Datasets and Network Architectures}
\label{sec:data}

S-MNIST presents each MNIST image~\cite{lecun1998gradient} as 784 scalar inputs, normalized with mean 0.1307 and standard deviation 0.3081. PS-MNIST uses a pixel permutation generated from the run seed and shared between training and test. Variability across seeds on PS-MNIST therefore includes changes in pixel order. Both tasks use two recurrent layers of widths 64 and 210. At evaluation, the linear readout averages logits over the full sequence.

QTDB uses four event streams derived from two ECG channels of the QT Database~\cite{laguna1997database}. The preprocessing code smooths each channel and thresholds its first differences at $\pm0.03$. The readout predicts six annotation groups at every timestep. Accuracy counts all positions in overlapping windows of length 1301 and stride 650. The generator lists record \emph{sel16483} in both partitions. The record membership of the preprocessed files is unverified, which limits interpretation of this score as generalization to unseen records.

GSC uses 12 classes from Speech Commands~\cite{warden2018speech}. Forty mel coefficients and their first and second differences give 120 features per frame. A 300-unit recurrent layer pools spikes over 101 frames for classification. SHD uses 700 cochlear input channels and 20 digit classes~\cite{cramer2022heidelberg}. A 64-unit recurrent layer drives 20 nonrecurrent SPRiF output units. The readout sums the softmax of their pre-reset membrane values over indices $t>10$.

\subsection{Training and Evaluation Protocols}
\label{sec:training}
\label{sec:statistics}

\label{sec:learning}

The forward pass uses a hard threshold. During backpropagation through time (BPTT), its derivative is replaced by the multi-Gaussian surrogate~\cite{asrnn}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\psi(v)=\gamma\big[&
a_0\mathcal N(v-\theta;0,\sigma_0^2)\\
&-a_1\mathcal N(v-\theta;\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)\\
&-a_1\mathcal N(v-\theta;-\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)\big],
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:surrogate}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal N(\cdot;\mu,\sigma^2)$ is the normalized Gaussian density,
$\sigma_0=0.5$, $\mu_1=0.5$, $\sigma_1=3.0$,
$a_0=1.15$, $a_1=0.15$, and $\gamma=0.5$.
Gradients pass through the recurrent spike and the membrane reset within each BPTT segment.

The benchmark runs use Adam for 150 epochs on S-MNIST, PS-MNIST and GSC, 250 on QTDB and 100 on SHD. The initial learning rate is 0.01 except on GSC, where it is 0.005. Batch sizes are 512 for the MNIST tasks, 128 for QTDB and GSC, and 100 for SHD. On both sequential MNIST tasks, each sequence is split into segments of 262, 262 and 260 steps. Each segment receives an optimizer update. State values carry over, and gradients stop at the boundaries. Within a segment, gradients pass through the spike and reset using Eq.~\eqref{eq:surrogate}. QTDB scores a 31-unit recurrent layer at each timestep. GSC uses negative log likelihood on the pooled readout with class-weighted sampling.

For S-MNIST, PS-MNIST, QTDB and SHD, each run contributes its highest test accuracy across epochs. The reported test split therefore also serves for checkpoint selection. GSC contributes its highest validation accuracy. SI-DMS uses the final checkpoint after 2000 updates.

Each benchmark decay is initialized from a positive time constant in discrete steps:
\begin{equation}
q=\exp(-1/\tau_q),\qquad q_{\rm raw}=\log\frac{q}{1-q}.
\label{eq:decay_init}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Baselines and Ablation Models}
\label{sec:controls}
\label{sec:baselines}

TC-LIF~\cite{tclif} and BRF~\cite{brf} were rerun on PS-MNIST and SHD. DMP-SNN~\cite{sun2026dmp} was rerun on PS-MNIST using its native architecture with layer-shared memory. Table~\ref{tab:main_results} marks our accuracies with an asterisk and lists separate parameter counts only when network sizes differ.

SPRiF, \mr{} and \rmodes{} have the same layer widths, five states and eleven learned dynamics coefficients per neuron on PS-MNIST and SHD. We use the same partitions, network readout, training budget, checkpoint rule and seed-specific inputs across variants. The two comparisons alter different terms in the drive to the membrane.

\noindent\textit{\mr.}
Compute the current spectral state $\mathbf x_t^-$, both readouts, the auxiliary state, and the membrane spike as in SPRiF. After firing, additionally update
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat B&=\frac{B}{\|B\|_2},\qquad \gamma_{\rm mem}=1,\\
\mathbf x_t^+&=\mathbf x_t^--\gamma_{\rm mem}\theta z_t\widehat B.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:memory_reset_control}
\end{equation}
The next update starts from $\mathbf x_t^+$. Because $h_t$ was computed from $\mathbf x_t^-$, the extra displacement enters the filter only at the following step. The reset has magnitude $\theta$ per spike and introduces no learned coefficient.

\noindent\textit{\rmodes.}
Replace the spectral matrices by
\begin{equation}
A_{\rm real}=\diag(a_1,a_2,a_3),\qquad
B_{\rm real}=\begin{bmatrix}1-a_1\\1-a_2\\1-a_3\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:real_control}
\end{equation}
where $a_i=\sigma(a_{i,\rm raw})$ are independently learned. Three real decay parameters replace $\alpha,\rho,\omega$. The auxiliary filter, membrane, scalar reset, and six readout entries are retained.

The first real-mode time constant uses the $\tau_\alpha$ interval, and the other two use independent draws from the $\tau_\rho$ interval. Each coordinate has a nonzero input coefficient. Simply setting the rotation angle to zero would leave one coordinate undriven from a zero initial state.

\subsection{SI-DMS Task and Intervention Protocol}
\label{sec:sidms}

SI-DMS is a binary match/non-match task. Each trial presents two 100-ms cues separated by a delay, following a 50-ms baseline. Thirty input channels encode the left cue, right cue and background in groups of ten. At each 5-ms step, active cue channels emit a Bernoulli spike with probability 0.20 and background channels with probability 0.05. A recurrent layer of 64 neurons drives two leaky outputs with a 20-ms time constant. The final outputs determine the class.

The training delays are 200, 400, 800 and 1600 ms. Clean training uses natural firing throughout. Intervention training samples $K\in\{0,1,2,4,8\}$ forced-spike times during the delay. At each selected time, ten of the 64 units (nominally 15\%) have their membrane candidates set to the current threshold plus 0.05. The induced spikes trigger each model's reset and enter the recurrent feedback. For SPRiF, the overwrite leaves the current spectral and auxiliary states intact. Training uses Adam with learning rate 0.003, batches of 256 and 2000 updates. Evaluation uses $K\in\{0,1,2,4,8,16,32,40\}$ and adds a 2500-ms delay. We present the nominal 15\% intervention condition, corresponding to ten of 64 neurons (15.625\%) at each selected timestep.

For seed $s$, delay $d$, count $K$, and fraction $f$, the paired loss is
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{s,d}(K,f)=
\Acc_{s,d}(0)-\Acc_{s,d}(K,f).
\label{eq:paired_loss}
\end{equation}
At $K=40$ and nominal $f=0.15$, we report the loss in percentage points over all five evaluation delays and separately at 2500~ms.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Temporal Classification Performance}

Table~\ref{tab:main_results} summarizes five temporal benchmarks. On PS-MNIST, SPRiF reached $95.86\pm0.14\%$ with 67,000 parameters, compared with $95.41\pm0.52\%$ for the native DMP-SNN rerun at the same reported size. On SHD, SPRiF reached $91.66\pm0.42\%$ with 50,000 parameters. The BRF and TC-LIF reruns reached $91.28\pm0.53\%$ and $88.73\pm0.44\%$ with 110,000 and 140,000 parameters, respectively. The controls below test how SPRiF uses its states on these two tasks while keeping layer width and local capacity fixed.

\subsection{Mechanistic Analysis and Ablation Studies}
\label{sec:temporal_responses}

Figure~\ref{fig:temporal_kernels} connects the spectral basis in Eq.~\eqref{eq:impulse} to the temporal responses on PS-MNIST, GSC and QTDB. The impulse responses show a real decay alongside damped oscillatory components (Fig.~\ref{fig:temporal_kernels}a). These components provide traces with different durations and phases for the membrane projections in Eq.~\eqref{eq:drive_kernel}. The frequency responses summarize the memory states across neurons in each task (Fig.~\ref{fig:temporal_kernels}b). Their shaded ranges describe variation across neurons. In the slow-decay comparison, the displayed SPRiF responses retain a larger normalized amplitude than ASRNN over the plotted interval on GSC and QTDB (Fig.~\ref{fig:temporal_kernels}c).

Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism} shows SPRiF memory states, membrane voltage and spikes around a recorded firing event on PS-MNIST. The memory trajectories continue through the marked event, while the membrane trace reflects firing and reset. This illustrates the separation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:sprif}: the spike changes the stored membrane voltage without subtracting from the spectral memory.

The two neurons have the same scalar membrane reset. In \mr{}, a spike at $t$ also contributes $r[1]=-\theta\mathbf g_0^\top A\widehat B$ to the drive at $t+1$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:reset_kernel}). The filter first carries part of that displacement to the membrane at $t+2$. Its sign depends on the learned projections. A memory reset thus adds a spike-dependent signal to the readout even if the incoming current is held fixed. SPRiF omits this path and scored 3.37 percentage points higher on PS-MNIST and 4.56 points higher on SHD (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

The mode comparison keeps the reset on the membrane. In \rmodes{}, the readout combines three driven exponential decays. In SPRiF, an input also leaves a trace whose direction rotates with elapsed time. Its projection can therefore weight successive phases differently under a common decay envelope (Eq.~\eqref{eq:impulse}). The real-plus-rotational block scored 2.34 percentage points higher on PS-MNIST and 2.47 higher on SHD than the three-real-mode block. This comparison tests the choice of basis and its associated input coupling together.

\subsection{Robustness to Spike Interventions}
\label{sec:sidms_results}

After clean training, SPRiF accuracy decreased from $99.99\%$ at $K=0$ to $99.42\%$ at $K=40$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:sidms_curves}). At $K=40$, \mr{} and \rmodes{} reached $94.74\%$ and $95.37\%$, while ASRNN, BRF and LIF ranged from $48.56\%$ to $54.80\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:sidms_results}).

After intervention training, SPRiF retained $99.33\%$ at $K=40$, a decrease of 0.36 percentage points from $K=0$. The matched controls had lower stressed accuracy.

The heatmaps show that SPRiF's mean accuracy exceeded $97.7\%$ at every displayed delay and intervention count. At the unseen 2500-ms delay and $K=40$, SPRiF reached $99.99\%$ after clean and $99.65\%$ after intervention training (Fig.~\ref{fig:sidms_heatmaps}). Clean-trained controls lost more accuracy at shorter delays.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

SPRiF gives the membrane a decaying trace and a phase-dependent trace of past input, each available through an immediate and a delayed projection. Each spike resets the membrane and can re-enter the spectral pathway one step later through recurrent current. Extending reset to spectral memory adds a distinct spike-history term to the drive. At fixed local capacity, both preserving that memory at discharge and retaining its rotational pair improved mean accuracy on PS-MNIST and SHD. On SI-DMS, SPRiF sustained accurate cue comparison under both training regimes at an unseen delay and intervention counts beyond training.
